BetterTanks: teaching a tank where to hide

The state machine took an afternoon. Getting the enemy to stop flickering between two hiding spots took most of the rest.

Context
Academic practice at DeviDevs, May–Jul 2026
Stack
Unity 2D, C#, Physics2D
Tracked
~87 h across 10+ Jira epics
Code
proiectul-Unity-2D

What it had to do

A top-down 2D tank game with destructible walls. The enemy tank needed to feel like it was making decisions rather than driving at you in a straight line: patrol when alone, engage when it sees you, and back off into cover when it is losing.

Five states, driven by health

EnemyAI.cs is a plain state machine — an enum, a switch in Update(), and one function that decides which state should be active: 

public enum State { Patrol, Chase, Shoot, Defensive, Flee }

What makes it behave less mechanically is that the transitions are driven by health rather than only by distance. GetDesiredState() checks, in order: below 25% health it flees, below 50% it goes defensive and looks for cover, otherwise it patrols or engages depending on whether it has detected you.

There is also a rule pointing the other way. If your health drops below 25%, the enemy gets more aggressive instead of more careful — it moves at 1.4× speed and its fire cooldowns are multiplied by 0.6 and 0.75. It presses the advantage.

Aggro is sticky and slightly unfair on purpose. Getting hit sets a provoked flag, and the enemy keeps chasing for six seconds after losing sight of you — except that it refuses to drop aggro at all while it is below the defensive threshold, because a wounded tank calmly going back to its patrol route looked absurd.

Choosing a cover position

This is the part I would want to be asked about. There are no hand-placed cover markers. For every destructible wall still standing, the AI derives four candidate positions from the collider's bounding box — one off each face: 

Bounds b = col.bounds;
Vector2[] candidates = {
    new Vector2(b.center.x, b.max.y + coverOffset),
    new Vector2(b.center.x, b.min.y - coverOffset),
    new Vector2(b.min.x - coverOffset, b.center.y),
    new Vector2(b.max.x + coverOffset, b.center.y),
};

Each candidate is scored. Being close to the enemy is worth ten times more than being far from the player, so it does not sprint across the map for a marginally better wall. Then the rule that actually matters: take the direction from the wall's centre to the player, and the direction from the wall's centre to the candidate, and compare them with a dot product. If the candidate is on the opposite side from the player (dot below −0.7), it gets a bonus of 1000 — large enough that geometry beats distance every time. A partially covered side (below −0.3) gets 300.

In other words: it does not know what "cover" is. It knows that the far side of a solid thing is worth a great deal more than the near side, and that falls out as hiding.

The three bugs that took the time

It vibrated between two walls

Re-scoring every frame meant two nearly equal walls would swap the top spot constantly and the tank would twitch in place. The fix is one line in the scoring loop: the wall it is currently using gets +50 before comparison. Hysteresis — it takes a meaningfully better option to justify moving.

It drove through the wall it was hiding behind

When you flank, the best position moves to the other side of the wall. The tank would head straight for it, which means straight into the wall it is standing against. So before switching sides it compares the current side and the target side; if they are opposed, it first sets a backoff target away from the wall centre, moves there, and only then goes around.

Its cover got destroyed mid-fight

Walls are destructible, so the thing it was hiding behind can disappear while it is using it. It now detects that, picks a retreat point away from the player, and reverses for a fixed 1.25 s while still shooting, instead of standing in the open re-running the search.

Obstacle avoidance

Movement goes through one helper. It raycasts along the desired direction; if that is blocked it tries the perpendicular, then the opposite perpendicular. If all three are blocked it flips the current dodge direction and returns zero, letting the caller stop rather than grind against geometry. Not a pathfinder — the maps are small and open enough that three raycasts are honest value for the cost.

What I would fix

Reading the code back now, three things are wrong with it:

  • Projectile hits are detected by name. CheckForProjectileHit does name.Contains("Bullet") and then excludes names containing "Muzzle" or "Flash". It works, and it breaks the moment someone renames a prefab. A layer or a component check is the right answer.
  • Cover search scans the whole scene. Every re-evaluation calls FindObjectsByType<DestructibleWall>. Fine at this map size, wrong as a habit — the walls should register themselves in a list once.
  • The object pool exists and the AI does not use it. There is an ObjectPool and a BulletPoolManager in the project, but EnemyAI.SpawnProjectile still calls Instantiate and Destroy on a timer. That was on my list when the practice period ended, and I would rather say so than claim the allocation problem is solved.

What I took from it

Behaviour that reads as intelligent came from scoring functions and hysteresis, not from anything clever. Almost every hour went into the transitions between states — leaving cover, losing cover, switching sides — rather than into the states themselves. And a debug line printing the current state and health percentage on every change was worth more than any amount of reading the code.
